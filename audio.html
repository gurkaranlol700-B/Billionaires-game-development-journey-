Seawall · Session 06

THE SOUND OF YOUR OWN BODY

Sound is roughly half of horror, and the corridor had none of it. This session built the audio system the whole game will use — and along the way discovered that 7.5 GB of free professional sound effects contained not one usable footstep, so the footsteps had to be built out of arithmetic instead.

Part one — one number instead of a hundred sounds

The obvious way to build horror audio is to place sounds: a drone here, a heartbeat there, a stinger on that trigger. It does not work, because fear is not a place. What makes Resident Evil Village and Outlast feel the way they do is that the sound reacts to your situation continuously, so your own body gives you away before you have seen anything at all.

So the system is built around a single number, Fear, between 0 and 1. The game feeds it — a scripted area, walking with the flashlight off, and from M2 the Watchman closing in — and the audio decides what that sounds like:

FearWhat the player hears
0.0Room tone, fluorescent hum, their own footsteps. No heartbeat at all
0.2The heart becomes audible, slow. A sub drone starts to sit under the room
0.5About 105 BPM, breathing shifts to the frightened set, a dissonant cluster fades in
1.0148 BPM, drone at full, the world itself ducked down — you hear yourself more than the building

Fear rises fast and falls slowly, which is how panic actually behaves: it arrives all at once and leaves reluctantly. One number, one rule, and every sound in the game can read it.

Why a subsystem

It lives in a UWorldSubsystem — one per level, created automatically, reachable from anywhere without wiring up a reference by hand. The Watchman will not need to know the audio exists; it will call ReportThreat and the mix will do the rest.

Part two — 7.5 GB of free sound, and not one footstep

Sonniss gives away a game audio bundle at GDC every year: royalty-free, commercial use allowed, no attribution required. The 2026 bundle is 7.47 GB and 347 WAV files. It was downloaded, extracted and catalogued with a small tool written for the job — it reads every file header and the broadcast-WAV description that libraries write their notes into, then sorts everything into the categories this game needs.

The catalogue is where the plan fell apart:

category          files   minutes
voice                59      26.4
sea_wind             41      33.9
electrical           33      20.5
door                 31      17.3
water                15      19.5
body_breath          14       3.7
footsteps            10       8.6   <- every one a false match

Those ten footsteps were a ticking clock, a pair of flip-flops, a metro station and a barber chair foot pump. Searching all 347 filenames for every footstep word, including the industry naming convention FOLYFoot, returned nothing usable. The bundle is a sampler: two to four files from each of 125 libraries, not the libraries themselves.

What it does contain is genuinely good for this island: Moaning Metal, Rocky Coast of Norway for the seawall, Fridge Hums, Sinister Textures, Ghostly Presences, bass drops, horn braams, colossal impacts, a haunted house kit, human effort and panting, and cloth foley. The older bundles ship complete libraries rather than samplers, so GDC 2019 — around 30 GB — is downloading for the next session.

Lesson

“7.5 GB of professional sound effects” and “the sounds this game needs” are unrelated statements. Read the file list before planning around a library — and when the file list is a page of marketing names, catalogue the files themselves.

Part three — sounds that cannot be recorded

Some of the most important sounds in horror do not exist as objects, so no microphone can capture them. They are built from arithmetic instead, which has the useful property that any of them can be re-tuned in seconds by changing a number.

SoundHow it is made
HeartbeatA lub and a dub, each a low tone whose pitch falls as it decays, plus a soft click. The falling pitch is what makes it a body rather than a drum machine. Three intensities
Sub droneTwo oscillators, 34 Hz and 34.37 Hz. That 0.37 Hz difference makes them beat slowly against each other, so the room feels wrong without anything being audible
Dissonant clusterA minor second and a tritone stacked, detuned and drifting — the “something is in here with you” layer
Shepard riserOctave-spaced partials sliding upward while a fixed curve fades out the top ones. It rises for twelve seconds and never arrives. This is the dizziness trick
StingersSub boom for the chest, inharmonic metal partials for the fear, noise slam for the transient
TinnitusTwo high tones and a narrow band of noise, decaying over six seconds — the ringing after a scare

Footsteps were supposed to be recordings. With none available they were synthesised from three stacked layers, which is also a fair description of what a real footstep is: 

transient   the heel striking stone        very short filtered noise burst
body        the slab answering back       low resonance, decays fast
grit        sand and dust under the sole  brief high noise, a few ms late

Change the balance of those three and the same code gives a careful crouch-walk, a normal walk, a run, a scuff, a push-off or a landing. Ten variations of each were generated — 70 files — because a footstep played from a single file is heard as a loop within about four steps.

Honest about this

These are placeholders with a purpose. They are not AAA foley, and real recordings will replace the files. But they let the whole footstep system be built, heard and tuned now, and when recordings arrive only the files change — not a line of code, not one asset reference.

Part four — the engine, not the sounds

The point of the session was never the sounds; it was the machine that plays them. Eight C++ classes, and every value they use sits in Project Settings › Game › Seawall Audio — 47 dials, tunable by ear while the game runs, with no recompile.

  • Sound sets — a footstep is not a file but eight takes, picked at random, never repeating the last three, with small random level and pitch. Everything in the game plays through this one object
  • Surfaces — a trace under the foot reads the physical material, so concrete, grit, puddles, metal grating and glass are different instruments. A box volume can override the floor as well, so a puddle needs no material of its own
  • Attenuation with occlusion — four presets for how a sound fades with distance and how much a wall muffles it. A sound through concrete must become dull, not merely quieter; that is what makes a building feel solid
  • Random emitters — a drip every 3.0 s at the same pitch becomes wallpaper in ten seconds. These randomise interval, position and pitch, and can be told to play only behind the player
  • Scare recipes — silence drop, stinger, follower footsteps, distant slam, light surge. Each is defined once in the director, so a scare is re-tuned in one place instead of twenty

The one worth describing is follower footsteps: the player's own step timings, replayed a fifth of a second late from several metres behind them, still walking for half a second after the player stops. It costs nothing, it uses sounds already loaded, and it is the most unpleasant thing in the build so far.

Part five — your heart does not stop when you do

The first version drove heart rate from the stamina bar, and it was wrong in a way that was obvious the moment it was heard: stamina refills in a few seconds, so sprinting and then standing still made the heartbeat fade almost immediately. Real bodies do not work like that. Heart rate peaks after you stop — which is exactly why slowing down feels worse than running. You were busy running; now there is nothing to do but listen to yourself.

So exertion became its own value, separate from the stamina the gameplay uses:

if (bSprinting)
{
    Exertion += ExertionRisePerSecond * DeltaTime;      // 0.30 per second
}
else
{
    if (bWasSprinting)
    {
        Exertion += ExertionSprintStopBump;            // +0.18 the frame you stop
    }
    Exertion -= ExertionDecayPerSecond * DeltaTime;     // 0.07 per second
}

The climb is four times faster than the recovery, and stopping gives it a kick. Sprint for four seconds and the heart is hammering; stop, and it climbs for another beat before it begins, slowly, to come down. The stamina bar still acts as a floor, so an empty bar always means a working body — but it no longer ends the sound the moment it refills.

Lesson

Reusing a gameplay number because it is already there is how audio ends up feeling like a user interface. Stamina is a rule; a heartbeat is a body. They had to be two values.

Part six — three traps that cost real time

1

The 5,605-byte zip file

Fixed

All five parts of the bundle downloaded in three seconds flat, at 5,605 bytes each. The server sits behind Cloudflare, which answers anything that does not look like a browser with a small 403 Forbidden web page — and the download tool obediently saved that page under the name of the zip.

plain request      HTTP/1.1 403 Forbidden   Content-Length: 5605
browser headers    HTTP/1.1 200 OK          Content-Length: 1301592146
Lesson

A download that “succeeded” is not a download that worked. Check the size, and check the first bytes — a zip begins with PK.

2

The download that had not stalled

Fixed

A 30 GB download appeared frozen: the file size had not moved in eight minutes, so it was killed and restarted with stall detection. Checked again, the file was at 173 MB — far past where it had supposedly stopped. Windows does not keep a file size up to date while another program is still writing to it. The download had been healthy the whole time.

Lesson

While something is being written, the size on disk is a rumour. Trust the writer log, not the file listing.

3

MetaSounds, and the door that opened instead

Changed

The plan was to build everything in MetaSounds, Unreal procedural audio graphs. Those graphs have to be authored, and automating the editor interface to do it proved unreliable. So the design changed: plain sound files driven by C++, which gives the same result for everything this game needs and removes a dependency outright. MetaSounds became an optional upgrade rather than a foundation.

The replacement turned out better than the original plan. Unreal has a Python plugin that can listen for commands over the local network — Remote Execution — so a script running outside the editor can reach into the live editor and build assets directly. That is how the 18 data assets, four attenuation presets and eight placed ambience actors in this session were made: by script, each value read back to confirm it, with nothing clicked by hand.

Lesson

When a plan depends on a fragile tool, the question is not how to automate the tool. It is whether the plan needs it at all.

Where things stand

  • Eight C++ audio classes, compiling: sound sets, surface map, settings, director, player body audio, surface volumes, random emitters, scare triggers
  • 86 sounds generated: heartbeats, tinnitus, drone, cluster, riser, stingers, body thuds, electrical arcs, and 70 footstep takes
  • 111 audio assets in the project, 18 of them data assets wired by script and verified
  • The corridor has room tone, six detuned fluorescent hums, a faulty fixture, electrical crackle, a dread zone and a stinger trigger
  • Heart rate and breathing driven by exertion and fear, not by the stamina bar
  • Real footsteps and breathing — the synthesised ones are placeholders, and the full GDC 2019 libraries are still downloading
  • Submixes and a master limiter, so the whole mix can be ducked and low-passed as one
  • Reverb per space — the corridor and the side rooms should not sound the same
  • Measuring the mix: recording the output and checking loudness, since the one thing that cannot be automated is listening
  • Next: the Watchman, which can already drive all of this through a single function call
The session in one line

Every sound made this session will eventually be replaced; the machine that plays them will not — which is why the machine, and not the sounds, was the work.
